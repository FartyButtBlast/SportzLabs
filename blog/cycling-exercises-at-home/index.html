Cycling Exercises at Home

A simple, no-fuss home strength routine for cyclists: build useful leg, trunk and upper-body strength without needing a gym.

By SportzLabs · 21 October 2026

You do not need a full gym to add useful strength work to your cycling week. A clear floor space, a sturdy chair or step, and optionally a resistance band or a pair of dumbbells are enough to get started.

The aim is not to turn every home session into a hard workout. It is to practise a few useful movements, build strength gradually and arrive at your key rides feeling capable rather than exhausted.

What home exercises can do for cyclists

Riding is excellent aerobic exercise, but it does not challenge every movement pattern or the upper body in the same way as resistance training. Strength work can support general strength and function. In cyclists, research on heavier strength training suggests potential benefits for cycling efficiency, anaerobic power and performance at a group level; it does not prove that one home routine works best for every rider.

At home, focus on movements you can control and repeat: a squat or split-squat pattern, a hip hinge or bridge, calf work, pushing, pulling where equipment allows, and trunk control.

A simple two-day home routine

Start with one or two non-consecutive sessions each week. Allow about 25–35 minutes. Finish most sets with two or three good repetitions still available; you should not need to work to failure.

Session A: legs and trunk

  • Chair squat or bodyweight squat: 2 sets of 8–12
  • Glute bridge: 2 sets of 8–12
  • Supported split squat or step-up: 2 sets of 6–10 each side
  • Calf raise: 2 sets of 10–15
  • Dead bug: 2 controlled sets each side

Session B: whole body

  • Step-up or reverse lunge: 2 sets of 6–10 each side
  • Hip hinge: 2 sets of 8–12 (use a backpack or dumbbells when ready)
  • Incline push-up: 2 sets of 6–12 (hands on a bench, table or wall)
  • Band or backpack row: 2 sets of 8–12
  • Side plank: 2 short, controlled holds each side

Use a wall, chair or railing for support with split squats and step-ups. It is better to make the movement stable and controlled than to make balance the whole challenge.

How to progress

For the first two or three weeks, learn the movements and notice how you feel over the next day or two. When every planned repetition feels controlled, add one or two repetitions. Once you reach the top of the range, make the movement a little harder: hold a light backpack, use a lower incline for push-ups, slow the lowering phase, or add a small amount of external load.

Change one thing at a time. A home plan works when it is repeatable, not when it leaves you too sore to ride well.

Where it fits around your rides

Protect your key interval session, long ride or event. You can put strength on another day, or ride first and complete a short strength session later the same day followed by an easy day. If your important rides repeatedly feel flat, reduce the number of sets before removing strength work entirely.

Technique cues that keep it simple

  • Squats and step-ups: keep your foot planted and let the knee travel in the same direction as your toes.
  • Hinges and bridges: move from the hips; stop before your lower-back position changes.
  • Push-ups and rows: choose an angle or resistance that lets your shoulders move comfortably.
  • Core exercises: aim for steady breathing and control, not a long hold with poor form.

When to get individual advice

New or worsening pain, dizziness, chest pain, fainting, unexpected breathlessness, recent surgery or a known condition that affects exercise are reasons to seek appropriate medical or rehabilitation advice. This guide is general education, not a diagnosis or personal clearance to train.

Key takeaways

  • One or two short home sessions a week is a sensible starting point.
  • Choose simple patterns you can control: squat, hinge, single-leg work, calves, push, pull and trunk control.
  • Progress gradually and leave a couple of repetitions in reserve.
  • Keep priority rides productive; adjust strength volume when cycling load rises.
