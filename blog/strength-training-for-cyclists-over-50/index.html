Strength Training for Cyclists Over 50

A straightforward two-day strength plan for cyclists over 50, with exercise options, practical progressions and recovery guidance that fits around your riding.

By SportzLabs · 19 October 2026

Strength training can be a useful addition to cycling after 50. The goal is not to chase exhaustion in the gym or copy a pro rider’s routine. It is to build and keep the strength, balance and confidence that support your riding and everyday life.

The most useful plan is one you can repeat. Start modestly, protect the rides that matter most to you, and change only one thing at a time.

Why add strength work?

Cycling is excellent aerobic exercise, but it does not load every part of the body in the same way as resistance training. Strength work can improve strength and physical function in older adults. Research in cyclists also suggests that heavier resistance training can improve cycling performance measures at a group level, although the evidence does not identify one proven programme specifically for riders over 50.

That means the sensible message is simple: lifting may help, but there is no magic age-specific exercise list. Choose movements you can perform comfortably, then progress gradually.

First, set a safe starting point

If you are returning after a long break, have osteoporosis, heart or neurological conditions, recent surgery, persistent pain, unexplained breathlessness, chest pain or fainting, speak with an appropriate health professional before starting a new training plan. A qualified coach or physiotherapist can also help adapt movements to your history.

For everyone else, begin below your limit. You should finish most sets feeling that you could have completed another two or three good repetitions. Sharp pain, increasing joint pain, dizziness or a major loss of control are reasons to stop and seek advice.

The simple weekly plan

Two non-consecutive sessions each week is a practical place to start. Keep each session to about 30–45 minutes. If that feels like too much alongside your riding, begin with one session for a few weeks.

  • Option 1: separate the hard days. Put strength on a different day from your most important interval session or long ride.
  • Option 2: group the stress. Ride first, then lift later the same day, followed by a truly easy day. This can work well when time is tight.

Neither option is automatically best. Use the one that leaves you able to complete your key rides with good form and energy. If the gym is consistently spoiling those rides, reduce gym sets before giving up strength work altogether.

Two full-body sessions

Do a short warm-up first: easy pedalling, a brisk walk, or a few minutes of gentle movement. Use a load you can control throughout the full range that is comfortable for you.

Session A

  • Squat pattern: goblet squat or leg press — 2 sets of 6–10 reps
  • Hip hinge: Romanian deadlift, kettlebell deadlift or a supported hinge — 2 sets of 6–10 reps
  • Pull: seated row or lat pull-down — 2 sets of 8–12 reps
  • Calves: standing or seated calf raise — 2 sets of 8–15 reps
  • Trunk: Pallof press or a farmer carry — 2 controlled efforts

Session B

  • Single-leg pattern: supported split squat or step-up — 2 sets of 6–10 reps each side
  • Hip extension: hip thrust or kettlebell deadlift — 2 sets of 6–10 reps
  • Push: incline push-up or dumbbell press — 2 sets of 8–12 reps
  • Calves: standing or seated calf raise — 2 sets of 8–15 reps
  • Trunk: side plank or dead bug — 2 controlled efforts

Machines are not second-best. A leg press or chest-supported row can be an excellent choice if it lets you train comfortably and progress safely. Use support for split squats or step-ups if balance, rather than leg strength, is the limiting factor.

How to progress without beating yourself up

Spend the first two to three weeks learning the movements and noticing how you feel over the following 24–48 hours. Keep just one or two work sets per exercise.

Once the movements feel steady and your rides are still going well, add a repetition within the suggested range. When you can complete the top of the range with good form, add a small amount of load and return to the lower end. You do not need to test a one-repetition maximum to make progress.

During a busy riding block, race period or a stressful week, keep the exercises familiar but trim the dose: remove optional extras first, then reduce a third set to two. Avoid introducing a new hard lift just before an event.

Recovery is part of the plan

Age is context, not a diagnosis. Some riders over 50 recover very quickly; others need more spacing between demanding sessions. Instead of relying on one watch score, look at the full picture: sleep, mood, energy, lingering soreness, joint comfort, recent training load and the quality of your next key ride.

Muscle soreness after a new exercise can be normal. Pain that changes your movement, gets worse or does not settle is not a cue to push through. Back off and get individual advice if needed.

What about intervals?

Strength training and cycling intensity can sit in the same plan, but they draw from the same recovery budget. Keep the hard bike work purposeful, leave easier rides easy, and avoid stacking hard sessions simply because a schedule says you should. Build an intensity block gradually, then review whether you are still sleeping well, enjoying your rides and completing the work you intended to do.

Key takeaways

  • Start with one or two full-body strength sessions a week, not a complicated gym programme.
  • Train useful movement patterns: squat, hinge, single-leg work, calf work, push, pull and trunk control.
  • Leave a couple of good repetitions in reserve; clean technique matters more than a heroic number.
  • Place strength around the rides that matter, then adjust based on how your body and bike sessions respond.
  • When your riding load rises, reduce gym volume before removing strength altogether.
