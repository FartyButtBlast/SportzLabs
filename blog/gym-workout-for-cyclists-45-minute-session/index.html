Gym Workout for Cyclists: Make a 45-Minute Session Count

A practical 45-minute gym workout for cyclists, with two weekly sessions, exercise substitutions and clear rules for choosing loads around your riding.

By SportzLabs · 16 November 2026

About a 10-minute read

You have finished work, your next important ride is on Thursday, and you have forty-five minutes in the gym. The challenge is deciding what deserves those minutes. A cyclist does not need to visit every machine or leave with legs that shake on the stairs. A useful session has a clear priority, enough rest to lift well and an exit point that protects the rest of the week.

This guide is about making a real gym visit work: selecting stations, choosing an appropriate load, dealing with occupied equipment and adjusting the session when riding has already taken its share of your energy. It is intended for adult recreational cyclists who can perform basic resistance exercises comfortably. It is not another long training block to add on top of everything you already do.

Each person's circumstances are different. Training history, health, injuries, available equipment and riding commitments change the right starting point. The sessions below are coaching examples, not personalised prescriptions. Ask a qualified exercise professional to teach unfamiliar lifts and adapt the plan for an existing condition. Stop for sharp pain, dizziness, chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness.

Decide what the session is for

Give the gym one main job today. During a quieter riding period that might be building lower-body strength. In a demanding riding week it might be maintaining familiar movements with less work. Before a major event, it might be a brief session early in the week or no lifting at all. Trying to build strength, improve aerobic fitness and test your toughness in the same visit usually makes the session harder to manage.

Research on heavy resistance training in cyclists supports potential performance benefits, although certainty is low and individual responses vary. It does not establish this particular workout as superior or promise an FTP increase. Treat the plan as an organised way to practise and progress, then judge it by your own training record. [Source]

Choose a primary leg exercise, a complementary movement and two supporting exercises. Four well-executed choices are enough to make the visit purposeful. An optional fifth exercise belongs at the end, where it can be removed without compromising the main work. This order also makes it easier to finish on time when the gym is busy.

A weekly layout around three rides

The example below assumes Tuesday is your interval day and Saturday is your longer ride. Move the days to match your actual commitments. The riding durations are examples for someone already comfortable with that workload, not instructions to increase your current cycling volume.

When combining sessions, separate them by several hours if practical, eat between them and place the priority workout first. If Tuesday's intervals leave you exhausted, do not force the planned lift. Use one gym session that week, or move the shorter session to a suitable day. The table is a starting arrangement, not proof that every cyclist should train twice on Tuesdays.

Use the first eight minutes properly

Spend three or four minutes walking or pedalling easily. Then rehearse the movements you will load: five comfortable squats, five hip hinges and a few easy arm pulls. This is preparation rather than a fitness test. You should reach your first working set more coordinated, without already feeling breathless.

For the primary exercise, perform two or three progressively heavier practice sets. If your working weight is a moderate dumbbell, that might mean a bodyweight rehearsal, a light dumbbell set and a short set with your intended weight. These do not count as working sets. Rest long enough to feel ready before the first recorded set.

A warm-up is also a decision point. Notice whether your usual movement feels comfortable and whether your legs feel merely slow or genuinely unwell. Persistent pain or deteriorating coordination is a reason to change the session. Adding more warm-up sets is not a reliable way to make a painful lift become appropriate.

Session A: one main lift, three supporting choices

Allow about forty-five minutes including preparation. Use the following order unless occupied equipment makes a small change necessary. The listed sets are working sets. Start with two sets of the first exercise if you are returning to the gym or uncertain about recovery.

Session A: one main lift, three supporting choices
ExerciseWorking prescriptionRestMain cue
Leg press3 × 6–82–3 minutesKeep the pelvis supported and use a comfortable depth
Dumbbell Romanian deadlift2 × 82 minutesMove the hips back; keep weights close
Chest-supported dumbbell row2 × 8–1090 secondsPull smoothly without lifting the chest off the bench
Standing cable Pallof press2 × 8 each side45–60 secondsPress forward without turning towards the cable

On the leg press, take time to adjust the seat and learn the safety mechanism. Lower only as far as you can control without the pelvis rolling away from the pad. A machine's printed weight is not comparable with another model. Record the machine, seat setting and load so next week's numbers mean something.

For the Romanian deadlift, soften the knees and let the hips travel backwards while the dumbbells stay near the legs. The movement ends when you reach your controlled hip range, not when the weights touch the floor. If you cannot distinguish a hip hinge from bending through the back, ask for instruction or use a familiar hip-thrust machine instead.

The row and Pallof press support a whole-body session without turning the last ten minutes into another leg workout. Use a cable resistance that allows quiet breathing and a steady stance. A heavier stack is not an improvement if you have to lean, twist or hold your breath to complete every repetition.

Session B: a smaller dose before the weekend

This visit is deliberately shorter, usually thirty to forty minutes. Its purpose is to practise unilateral loading and include pushing and calf work while keeping the weekend manageable. Do not turn it into a second maximal leg day because Session A felt productive.

Suitcase carry2 × 20–30 seconds each side60 secondsLighter weight and a shorter clear walkway

For the split squat, use a rail or rack upright for balance. Keep both feet on the floor, lower vertically between them and use the front leg to rise. Support is helpful when the goal is leg loading rather than balance practice. Start each set with the less confident side and match the same controlled repetitions on the other side.

The carry should look like ordinary walking with a manageable weight in one hand. Keep your steps natural and avoid leaning away from the load. If the floor is crowded, a stationary suitcase hold is a sensible alternative. Set the weight down before grip failure makes the return to the rack awkward.

Choose a load you can repeat

For most working sets here, finish with approximately two or three good repetitions still available. This is often described as repetitions in reserve. It is an estimate, not a laboratory measurement. New lifters should err towards a lighter load while learning what a challenging but controlled set feels like.

Suppose your leg-press target is three sets of six to eight. You complete eight, seven and six with consistent technique. Keep the same weight next time. If you complete eight on all three sets and still have the intended reserve on two visits, consider the smallest available increase. Return towards six repetitions and build again.

Do not increase the weight, add another set and shorten rest in the same session. You would make three changes without knowing which one caused the extra fatigue. A clear progression rule gives the gym visit direction while leaving room for riding, sleep and normal life to affect the pace of improvement.

What to do when equipment is occupied

Replace the movement with a familiar option serving the same broad purpose. A goblet squat can substitute for the leg press. A seated cable row can replace the supported dumbbell row. A cable chest press can replace the chest-press machine if you know its setup. Record the substitution rather than comparing the weights as though they were identical.

Avoid learning a technically demanding barbell exercise simply because the easier station is busy. If a replacement needs coaching, choose something you already understand for today. Consistency includes making safe practical decisions in imperfect gyms, not defending one exercise list at all costs.

You can pair the upper-body row with the Pallof press to save time, provided each exercise still gets sufficient rest. Do not rush back and forth between the two demanding leg movements. Breathlessness from a circuit can hide how much muscular effort remains, making it harder to choose useful loads.

A four-week trial with clear review points

Use this short trial to assess whether the sessions fit your riding. It is not a promise that four weeks produces a particular performance change. Repeat a week when work, travel or recovery interrupts the intended progression.

WeekSession ASession BReview question
1Two sets per exercise; conservative loadsOne or two sets per exerciseWere the next rides comfortable?
2Use the full listed sets if recovery was goodKeep the same exercises and loadsAre setup and technique more repeatable?

If your Saturday ride suffers twice after Thursday strength, first remove a set from the split squat or make Thursday upper-body only. If that is insufficient, use one weekly gym visit for now. The useful dose is the one you can recover from, not the largest dose that fits in a calendar.

Record enough to make the next visit easier

Imagine arriving on Thursday after a harder-than-usual group ride the previous evening. Your warm-up feels heavy, but the upper body feels normal. Use the chest press and carry as planned, then try one easy split-squat set. If the legs still feel poorly coordinated, omit the remaining leg work. Record the decision and assess Friday rather than forcing the original dose.

That is different from abandoning the programme whenever enthusiasm is low. A planned adjustment has a reason, a specific change and a review point. Over several weeks, those notes may reveal that Wednesday's group ride is the real scheduling conflict. Moving the smaller gym session or reducing that ride can then solve the recurring problem more effectively than repeatedly changing exercises.

Log the exercise, load, completed repetitions and any setup detail you would otherwise forget. Add one sentence about how the session affected the next ride. “Legs normal by Friday; Saturday climbs felt usual” is more useful than a soreness score with no context. Avoid treating ordinary day-to-day variation as evidence that the entire programme has failed.

Save the two sessions as reusable routines in Workout Studio. Review several weeks together before making major changes. The goal is a gym session you understand well enough to adapt: arrive with a priority, perform the important work, and leave with enough capacity to enjoy being a cyclist.
